Finding and Treating Water in the Wild

Learn how to assess a water source, remove sediment and choose a treatment method without confusing clear water with safe water.

A clear stream is not automatically safe to drink. Water can contain bacteria, viruses, parasites or chemical contaminants that are invisible and have no obvious taste or smell. The safest plan is to carry enough known-safe water for your route and treat natural water only when necessary.

1. Choose the source carefully

Avoid water downstream of settlements, farms, mines, roads, campsites or industrial activity. Stay clear of stagnant pools, algal blooms, animal carcasses and obvious runoff. Heavy rain can increase contamination. Even a remote-looking source can carry pathogens from wildlife or upstream activity.

2. Remove visible particles first

If water is cloudy, let sediment settle and carefully pour the clearer water into another container, or pre-filter it through a clean cloth or suitable pre-filter. This improves clarity but does not disinfect the water or make it safe on its own.

3. Boiling: a dependable method for germs

Bring clear water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute. At elevations above 6,500 feet (about 2,000 metres), boil for 3 minutes. Let it cool and store it in a clean, covered container. Avoid re-contaminating it with untreated hands, bottles or utensils.

4. Filters and chemical treatment

Portable filters differ. Check the manufacturer's tested claims: many remove protozoa, some remove bacteria, and many do not remove viruses. Some situations require filtration followed by a correctly dosed disinfectant. Follow product instructions and local health guidance rather than assuming every filter does everything.

Important: chemicals are different. Boiling, ordinary filters and disinfection do not reliably remove fuel, pesticides, heavy metals or other toxic chemicals. If chemical contamination is possible, do not use the source; find another safe supply.

Source: CDC — Water treatment for hiking, camping and travel.
